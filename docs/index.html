The recent news of suspected cable sabotage in the Baltic Sea, along with historical precedents like the 2008 Mediterranean cuts and the 2023 Red Sea attacks, demonstrates the reality of these risks. The concentration of cables in narrow passages makes them susceptible to both accidental damage (anchor drags, fishing) and intentional attacks. The acquisition of a fiber pair on Google’s Echo Subsea Cable by FLAG also suggests a commercial aspect to cable access and control. Given that over 95% of intercontinental data traffic relies on these cables, disruptions at these chokepoints can have significant ripple effects on global commerce and security. A coordinated attack targeting multiple chokepoints simultaneously could cripple internet connectivity across entire regions.

Energy & Supply Chain (PIR 3)

The analysis of power and cooling constraints reveals critical vulnerabilities in the AI infrastructure supply chain. The Global Data Center Density and AI Compute Concentration report emphasizes that newer data centers are increasingly power-dense due to AI workloads, demanding massive power and space. The planned AWS Susquehanna Campus, intended to be nuclear-powered, highlights the growing energy demands of hyperscale computing. The concentration of AI compute in a few countries creates a 'Compute Divide,' leaving the majority of nations without the infrastructure for large-scale AI. This divide exacerbates existing economic and technological disparities, making those nations dependent on the compute resources of others. Kinetic vulnerabilities include the physical security of substations and water supply infrastructure. Disruptions to these critical resources could significantly impact the operation of AI data centers, particularly in regions with limited redundancy or alternative supply sources. The trend of plugging containerized data centers into local heating networks, as seen in Finland, also presents potential dependencies and vulnerabilities, particularly if the heating network itself becomes a target.
