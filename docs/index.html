Maritime Resilience (PIR 1)

The subsea cable network is inherently vulnerable due to its reliance on a limited number of physical pathways and geographic chokepoints. As stated in Global Undersea Cable Chokepoints..., over 95-99% of intercontinental data traffic relies on these cables, and disruptions can have significant ripple effects. Key chokepoints include the Bab-el-Mandeb Strait, Strait of Malacca, and Strait of Florida. The news of potential cable sabotage in the Baltic Sea and the actual Red Sea attacks highlight the real-world nature of these threats. Even a seemingly minor incident can slow stock exchanges and payment systems, as seen in past cable outages. The increasing number of active cables (over 570 as of 2025) concentrates risk in areas where these cables converge, such as landing station clusters in Asia-Pacific, Europe, and North America. The SEA-ME-WE 5 cable landing in Egypt, for instance, represents a single point of failure affecting Europe-to-Asia connectivity.

Frontier AI Geopolitics (PIR 2)

The strategic placement of new AI and hyperscale facilities reveals a growing competition for AI dominance, primarily between the US and China, but with the rest of the world in support roles. According to Global Data Center Density..., the US holds over half of the world's hyperscale data centers. This asymmetry is growing, with the US widening its lead in hyperscale infrastructure. The planned buildout of AI training centers in the US (e.g., 365 Data Centers/Aphorio Carter, Core Scientific Muskogee) indicates a focus on maintaining AI leadership. China's focus on domestic edge buildout mentioned in Global Evolution of Compute Infrastructure... may reflect a strategy of self-reliance. This is also consistent with the Digital Silk Road vision, where China seeks to establish its digital infrastructure footprint across Asia and Africa. The planned Google Subsea Cable Project (Thailand) could be related to expanding cloud infrastructure into regions along the Digital Silk Road. The trend of shifting compute to private AI clusters, especially in the US, indicates a desire to control AI infrastructure.

Energy & Supply Chain (PIR 3)

The increasing demand for AI compute is putting significant strain on power and cooling infrastructure. Generative AI applications require massive GPU clusters, demanding extreme power and space, as highlighted in Global Data Center Density.... The planned nuclear-powered cloud (AWS Susquehanna Campus) suggests a recognition of the growing energy demands of hyperscale computing. Cooling, particularly in dense urban areas, adds another layer of complexity. The 'Compute Divide' means that most countries lack the infrastructure for large-scale AI compute, creating asymmetric vulnerabilities. Kinetic vulnerabilities include substations supplying power to data centers and water supplies for cooling. An attack on these critical nodes could cripple AI capabilities disproportionately affecting countries with high AI compute concentration. The T.Loop Data Center in Finland and the Vatajankoski projects are an example of creative solutions that may be necessary to meet these increasing demands.
