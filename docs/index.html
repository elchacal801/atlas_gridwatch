Maritime Resilience (PIR 1)

The subsea cable network faces significant physical resilience challenges due to geographic chokepoints and the concentration of cables in specific areas. The 'Global Undersea Cable Chokepoints and Landing Clusters: A Strategic Risk Assessment' highlights several critical vulnerabilities. For example, the Bab-el-Mandeb Strait is a bottleneck for Europe-to-Asia cables, making it a high-risk zone. The Strait of Malacca also poses risks due to heavy shipping traffic and shallow channels, increasing the likelihood of anchor drags or fishing-related damage. Historically, the 2008 Mediterranean cable cuts and the 2023 Red Sea attacks demonstrate the potential for disruptions to sever entire regions' connectivity. Recent news of Finnish authorities investigating suspected cable sabotage in the Baltic Sea underscores the ongoing threat of intentional damage. Redundancy is also a concern, as cables concentrate along high-capacity 'trunk' routes, meaning failures can have outsized ripple effects. The Strait of Florida is another high-density corridor, with multiple systems connecting South Florida to Latin America, creating a potential single point of failure. Furthermore, the Isthmus of Panama concentrates cables bridging the Pacific and Caribbean, making it a strategic junction. These vulnerabilities are exacerbated by the relatively shallow burial depth of many cables, making them susceptible to both accidental and intentional damage.

This dominance allows the US to control a larger share of the AI development and deployment landscape. Energy & Supply Chain (PIR 3)

Power and cooling are critical constraints for data centers, especially high-density AI compute facilities. The 'Global Data Center Density and AI Compute Concentration: Trends and Geopolitical Implications' notes that newer facilities are increasingly power-dense, often exceeding 100+ MW campuses due to AI workloads. This reliance on massive power infrastructure creates kinetic vulnerabilities, particularly at substations and water supply systems. Disruption to these critical utilities could cripple data center operations. The increasing demand for energy also raises concerns about sustainability and reliance on potentially unstable grids. The 'Compute Divide', where a small number of countries possess the majority of AI compute infrastructure, exacerbates these vulnerabilities. Nations lacking substantial compute resources are more reliant on external providers, making them susceptible to supply chain disruptions and potential geopolitical leverage. Edge deployments, as discussed in 'Global Evolution of Compute Infrastructure toward Edge', aim to mitigate some of these dependencies by distributing compute resources closer to end-users, but they still rely on underlying power and connectivity infrastructure. The planned 400MW data center in Eastern South Africa highlights the growing need for robust energy infrastructure to support AI expansion in developing regions. The concentration of AI power in certain regions can lead to an imbalance, where other regions are heavily dependent on those with robust compute infrastructure and sustainable supply chains.
