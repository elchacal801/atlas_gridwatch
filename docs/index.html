Energy & Supply Chain (PIR 3)

Data centers, especially those dedicated to AI training, are highly dependent on stable power and cooling infrastructure. The increasing power density of these facilities, driven by the use of GPUs and accelerators, demands massive power and space, as mentioned in "Global Data Center Density and AI Compute Concentration: Trends and Geopolitical Implications" (ID: 2025-global-compute.md). Power constraints can limit the scalability of AI infrastructure, particularly in regions with unreliable energy grids. Cooling requirements also pose a significant challenge, necessitating access to large volumes of water or advanced cooling technologies. Kinetic vulnerabilities, such as substations and water supply infrastructure, represent potential points of failure. A coordinated attack on these critical infrastructure components could severely disrupt data center operations. The "compute divide" exacerbates these vulnerabilities, as countries lacking sufficient AI infrastructure are increasingly reliant on a few dominant providers, making them susceptible to disruptions in those providers' supply chains or energy sources. The trend towards edge computing, as highlighted in "Global Evolution of Compute Infrastructure toward Edge" (ID: 2026-01-04-global-compute-evolution.md), may partially alleviate these dependencies by distributing compute resources closer to end-users, but it also introduces new challenges related to security and management of distributed infrastructure.
