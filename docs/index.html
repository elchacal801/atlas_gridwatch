Maritime Resilience (PIR 1)

The subsea cable network faces significant physical resilience challenges. The 'Global Undersea Cable Chokepoints and Landing Clusters: A Strategic Risk Assessment' highlights numerous geographic chokepoints where cable disruptions can have outsized effects. The Bab-el-Mandeb Strait, a critical passage between Europe and Asia, is a prime example, with nearly a dozen major cable systems converging there. A single well-placed attack or accident could sever connectivity between these continents. The Strait of Malacca, another key chokepoint, is vulnerable to anchor drags and fishing activities. Historical precedents, such as the 2008 Mediterranean cuts and the 2023 Red Sea attacks, demonstrate the real-world impact of cable outages. Recent news of the Finnish police investigating suspected cable sabotage in the Baltic Sea further underscores the persistent threat. The concentration of cables in landing clusters also presents a vulnerability. Any disruption, whether accidental or intentional, at these landing points can cause widespread outages. Given that the undersea cable network carries over 95% of intercontinental data traffic, any successful attack or disruption could have devastating consequences for global communications and commerce.

The 'Digital Silk Road' initiative aligns with China's efforts to expand its digital influence, potentially challenging the U.S.'s dominance in the long term. The concentration of AI compute power in the hands of a few nations, especially the U.S. and China, creates an asymmetry in capability. Furthermore, the planned buildout of facilities like the 'OpenAI Stargate UAE' indicate a broader geographic distribution and potential power projection via AI infrastructure in strategically important regions.

Energy & Supply Chain (PIR 3)

Power and cooling constraints are critical vulnerabilities in the supply chain. AI data centers require massive amounts of energy and advanced cooling systems. Disruptions to power grids or cooling infrastructure (e.g. water supply) could cripple these facilities. 'Global Data Center Density and AI Compute Concentration: Trends and Geopolitical Implications' notes that generative AI is driving the deployment of unprecedented clusters of GPUs, which demand massive power and space. This increased demand can strain existing infrastructure and create dependencies on specific suppliers. The 'compute divide,' where only a few countries hold the lion's share of AI-oriented computing power, exacerbates these vulnerabilities. Nations lacking sufficient infrastructure are dependent on others, potentially creating chokepoints in the AI supply chain. Kinetic vulnerabilities, such as substations and water supplies, represent significant risks. An attack on these physical components could have cascading effects, disrupting data center operations and impacting the broader digital ecosystem.
