Maritime Resilience (PIR 1)

The undersea cable network faces significant physical resilience challenges due to its concentration in geographic chokepoints. The Bab-el-Mandeb Strait, Strait of Malacca, and the Strait of Hormuz are critical vulnerabilities, as highlighted in "Global Undersea Cable Chokepoints and Landing Clusters: A Strategic Risk Assessment". A disruption in these areas, whether accidental or intentional, can sever major arteries of global data flow between Europe, Asia, and Africa. The recent Finnish investigation of suspected cable sabotage underscores the acute risk of state or non-state actors targeting these vulnerabilities. Historical precedents, such as the 2008 Mediterranean cable cuts and the 2023 Red Sea attacks, demonstrate the potential for significant regional outages. The concentration of cables in landing clusters further exacerbates this risk, as damage to a single landing station can impact multiple cable systems. The rise of private hyperscaler cable systems adds another layer of complexity, potentially creating new dependencies and vulnerabilities if these systems lack sufficient redundancy or are not adequately protected.

The U.S. currently dwarfs all other countries and regions. This expansion is not merely about economic growth but also about securing a strategic advantage in AI capabilities. China's "Digital Silk Road" initiative aims to establish a parallel digital infrastructure, potentially challenging U.S. dominance. The concentration of AI compute power in a few countries, primarily the U.S. and China, creates an asymmetry that could exacerbate existing geopolitical tensions. The planned buildout of AI facilities in locations like the UAE (OpenAI Stargate UAE) underscores the growing importance of strategic alliances and access to resources in the AI race. Furthermore, the ongoing expansion of edge computing infrastructure, as outlined in "Global Evolution of Compute Infrastructure toward Edge", also plays into this competition, with both the U.S. and China vying to establish a distributed network of AI-capable devices and micro-data-centers.

Energy & Supply Chain (PIR 3)

Data centers, particularly those supporting frontier AI, face significant supply chain and energy dependencies. The demand for power and cooling is immense, with new facilities requiring substantial energy infrastructure upgrades. Kinetic vulnerabilities include substations and water supply, which can be targeted to disrupt operations. The "Compute Divide" described in "Global Data Center Density and AI Compute Concentration: Trends and Geopolitical Implications" highlights the fact that only a small number of countries possess the infrastructure for large-scale AI compute, creating a dependency on these nations. The announcement of nuclear-powered cloud facilities (AWS Susquehanna Campus) indicates the lengths to which companies are willing to go to secure reliable power sources. Securing access to rare earth minerals and advanced chip manufacturing capabilities is also critical, creating potential bottlenecks and dependencies. The planned Nostrum Data Centers in Spain, aiming for an "AI-ready data center platform", further illustrates the scramble to provide specialized infrastructure and energy solutions for AI workloads.
